Life aboard: the research

Status: 2026-11-13 (D20). Three research passes ran in parallel for the owner's new direction (life-aboard.md): how games build and live on a ship or mobile base, how games assign named characters to duties, and how games do relationships, light romance, decorated quarters and bond-driven combat. Each read official pages, wikis, reviews and design talks. Several wikis (Fandom, GamePress, Koumakan, Prydwen) blocked direct reads, so some facts come from their search extracts, cross-checked where possible; numbers in live games change with patches, so treat them as snapshots. Anything not verified is said so. The design's calls are TC55–TC74.

The short answer

  • Ships: the strongest patterns are one visible budget (FTL's reactor, XCOM 2's power, Starfield's classes), a base that grows as people join (Suikoden, Skies of Arcadia), crew at stations giving ship-wide effects (FTL, Skies of Arcadia), and the ship in battle as a few capped calls (Valkyria Chronicles 4's Ship Orders, the closest model to this game). The payoffs: upgrades tested at a finale (Mass Effect 2) and your own ship as a battle map (XCOM 2's Avenger Defense). Avoid upkeep, gathering minigames, maintenance that punishes, and walking hubs.
  • Duties: rooms keyed to one stat are the most legible match (Fallout Shelter, Uma Musume's own training facilities). Real-time timers draw the most complaints in single-player games (Dragon Age: Inquisition's war table). Every base that launched without auto-assign and presets added them later (Arknights, Girls' Frontline, Metal Gear Solid V). Thresholds beat sums for keeping spreadsheets away.
  • Bonds: a ranked meter with scenes at thresholds (Persona, Fire Emblem, NIKKE), fed by time, gifts and fighting together, paying out in battle as small stats, reactive moments or shared actions (Persona's Follow Up and Endure, XCOM 2's bonds, Chrono Trigger's techs). Romance as a branch at the top of friendship, with equal rewards (Dragon Age II, Fire Emblem Engage's platonic S-supports), avoids the pressure to romance. Decoration is a comfort score feeding a passive benefit (Blue Archive, Arknights, Azur Lane). The pitfalls: tone that ages badly, gift grind, jealousy, and punishing neglect, which with a hundred crew becomes a hundred chores.

Patterns:

  1. One budget with visible pips forces readable trade-offs.
  2. The base grows as people join.
  3. Crew on stations give ship-wide effects.
  4. The ship shows up in battle as a few capped calls.
  5. Upgrades pay off at a finale.
  6. Upgrades open the map.
  7. Rewards refresh per battle, not per hour.
  8. The ship itself as a battle map.
  9. Personal touches make it home.

Pitfalls: real-time timers and dispatch chores; upkeep that becomes a clock; gathering minigames to pay for upgrades; maintenance punishments; walking hubs and action points; trap modules; slow travel and forced encounters on the way; simulation depth that needs long sessions.

Patterns:

  1. Rooms keyed to a stat are the most legible match.
  2. Unique per-character perks give named characters a life outside battle, and cause most of the min-maxing.
  3. Opportunity cost runs from none (Genshin) to a hard lock (Girls' Frontline); the middle is best.
  4. Recovery as a room gives the bench a purpose.
  5. Three time bases (real time, in-game time, per battle); real time draws the most complaints.
  6. Growth by doing the job.
  7. Pairing at a task builds relationships.
  8. Duty output often buys a one-battle edge.
  9. Automation added after launch shows where the chore was.
  10. Thresholds beat linear sums.

Pitfalls: real-time timers and appointments; collecting by hand; a second fatigue meter needing rotation; synergy webs that need spreadsheets; dispatch with no cost, or a lock that steals the squad; activities detached from the characters; a fixed hub loop that drags; hidden math; punishing failure on passive duty.

Patterns:

  1. A ranked meter with scenes at thresholds.
  2. Three inputs: time together, gifts by preference, fighting side by side.
  3. Five strengths of battle payoff, from small stats to a unique power.
  4. Gates: a story chapter, a personal mission, a key item.
  5. Romance as a branch at the top of friendship.
  6. Decoration as a comfort score capped by a facility's level.
  7. Matching her taste.
  8. A time budget per cycle.
  9. A messaging app for cheap contact.
  10. Endings that reflect bonds.

Pitfalls: tone that ages badly, and a captain courting subordinates; grind and gift spam; romance worth more than friendship; jealousy mechanics; punishing neglect; missable scenes; a writing budget of a hundred crew times their scenes.
