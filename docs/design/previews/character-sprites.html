Character looks and sprites: the authoring spec

Status: 2026-11-13 (D23), for the owner's review. The owner asked for the character art in the Uma Platform app's Character Art (the Cyberpunk universe's approved looks of the first two pilot Umas) to inform the game's looks, and for a spec of how the character sprites are authored from that art and Triangle Strategy's camera. This page is the game's side, nameless: Umas are outfit ids, and the per-Uma notes are private (content/design/character-looks/). Its calls are LK1–LK14, each a recommendation, adopted until the owner changes one. The sprite format it builds on is uma-platform's (format.md, v1); what it needs from uma-platform is a request. Visual page: previews/character-sprites.html.

The short answer

  • Five looks per Uma, each tied to where she is (LK1): Street when exploring worlds and cities, Quarters in her cabin and her evenings aboard, Pilot suit at her ship station, Combat armor on the battle board, and the helmet closed only when a map is sealed or for the cut-in's strike.
  • One template per look, shared by every Uma (LK3): the same jacket cut, plugsuit, Modular Frame armor and Helmet, personalised by her palette, her emblem and her head (hair, ears, eyes, accessory). That's what the ten approved looks already do, and it's how a hundred Umas stay consistent and affordable.
  • Two kinds of character art, split by camera (LK4):
    • Board sprites stand in the HD-2D diorama: small, four facings, few frames, read at about 69 pt on the phone.
    • Portraits and cut-in keys carry the drama: the approved full-body looks as portraits in scenes, visits and

    menus, and a few large side-view combat keys for the cut-in. Fire Emblem splits its art the same way (map sprites, battle animations, portraits), and Triangle Strategy and Octopath draw their portraits apart from their sprites.

  • The board's camera is ours, not Triangle Strategy's (LK5): Triangle Strategy's camera turns a full circle, which had its team build every map for every side. Ours is uma-stage's orthographic view, fixed at 35°, along the grid's axes, so a sprite needs four facings (front, back, left, right), never diagonals, and nothing rotates.
  • The sprite (LK6–LK9): uma-platform's 256-texel cell and foot point kept, a standing Uma about 212 texels tall, adult HD-2D proportions of about four heads (the platform's chibi sets are three), redrawn at sprite size, never shrunk from the full body, light from the top front-left, never mirrored. A small set per look, about 64 frames for combat and fewer elsewhere, with held-back motion; the helmet as a head patch, not a second set.
  • Authoring (LK10–LK13): ChatGPT draws the character art (uma-platform P126, P147) from the approved looks; the pilots' combat set first, then their station, quarters and exploration sets; then the other leads. The game picks the look by context and falls back to the original-outfit chibi set or a face card while a look isn't drawn.

1. What the approved looks establish

The Cyberpunk universe in Uma Character Art (published 2026-11-13, style "Hardline Anime", revision 3) holds ten approved full-body looks, 1024 × 1536: five looks each for the two pilot Umas (outfits 106801 and 112901).

Look (its id in the art)Its title thereThe template
streetStreetA cropped jacket or shell over a top with her emblem, cargo pants with knee pads, fingerless gloves, high-top sneakers
quartersQuarters: pajama shorts and crop topA crop top with her emblem, drawstring shorts, slippers
pilotShip operating: pilot suitA skintight plugsuit in her palette, line panels, clamps or knots at the hips, gloves, boots
armorCombat armor: Modular Frame 01Chest plate with her emblem, segmented abdomen, big shoulder pads, forearm gauntlets, thigh, knee and shin plates, armored boots; head bare
armor-helmetHelmeted combat armor: Helmet 01The same armor and a full helmet with armored ear housings, a dark visor band and her emblem on the forehead

Across her five looks, these never change: her hair, ears, eyes, tail and signature head accessory (except under the helmet), her emblem (carried onto every outfit), and her palette. The tail is always out; the ears always show (the helmet houses them). Every look is asymmetric (a hair ornament on one side, a braid, a crest on one sleeve, a shield on one forearm), so sprites are never mirrored.

There's precedent in part: Octopath shows a character's second-job outfit only in battle and keeps her own clothes on the field, while Triangle Strategy keeps one sprite everywhere and changes it on a promotion. This game goes further, because its looks are the life aboard made visible: she dresses for where she is.

LK2, the context decides, a scene can override. The game picks by context: a world location is Street, her cabin and the ship's spots are Quarters, a post is Pilot suit, a mission is Combat. A scene or bond scene can name a look (look in the story format, live with the packet that first plays looks) when the story wants another, such as her pilot suit in a Bridge emergency at night.

The helmet rule (part of LK1): on the board the helmet stays off by default, because her face, hair and accessory are how she's told apart at 69 pt. It closes on sealed maps (a map flag, every Uma at once, so it's a visible "this place is hostile" signal), for the suit-up's last beat, and on the cut-in's strike. On a sealed map her identity rests on her helmet's palette, ear housings and emblem, her ring's colour, and her face on the timeline chip and unit card, which stay helmet-off. Her rig's Visor part (TC17) recolours the helmet's visor band, so the part reads on the helmet.

3. The template system (LK3)

LK3, five templates for every Uma, personalised three ways. The approved looks already work this way: both pilots wear the same jacket cut, plugsuit, Modular Frame and Helmet.

Palette—Three roles: base (the largest areas), accent (her identity colour, TC41's hue), trim (piping, buckles, glow); taken from her original outfit

What it buys: a new Uma's five looks are one consistent family; a board of twelve armored Umas reads as a squad, each told apart by head and accent; and the art can be checked against a fixed list (does the emblem sit where the template puts it, is the accent hers).

4. Two kinds of character art (LK4)

LK4, board sprites for the diorama, portraits and keys for the drama. Fire Emblem pairs its map sprites with separate battle animations and portraits, and Unicorn Overlord cuts from small map figures to side-on fighters. Triangle Strategy and Octopath draw their portraits apart from their sprites (a Triangle Strategy mod swaps one heroine's portrait on its own), and Octopath's sprites were converted by hand from the illustrations, finished to look best as sprites with stronger colours. This game does the same, with the cut-in's keys as the battle close-up.

5. The camera (LK5)

LK5, the board's camera is uma-stage's, fixed. Triangle Strategy's camera is a reference for the look, not the geometry:

Triangle StrategyThis game's board (uma-stage)
RotationA full 360° on the right stick, the big change from Octopath's fixed camera; its team built map parts for every direction and argued long over the map edgesNone (TC16, TC48: 90° steps only if a map ever needs them)
Tilt and zoomZoom on the shoulder buttons in battle and in town; the view tilts from near isometric to nearly top-downFixed pitch; a two-axis pan, no zoom in v1
ProjectionNot published; reads as a narrow-angle perspectiveOrthographic
PitchNot published; reads as about 30–40° at the default view (Final Fantasy Tactics' usual camera is about 26.5°)35°, fixed
Grid on screenAny angle, since the camera turnsAxis-aligned: rows run across the screen, the back row at the top
Facing as a ruleFour grid facings, chosen as a turn ends; a hit from behind is a criticalFour, the same (TC23: side +25%, back +50%), shown as a wedge on the base ring
Views drawnNot published; with a free camera, the view shown follows her facing against the camera's angleFront, back, left, right (three-quarter turned), each seen from one angle only
ScaleNot published; a unit about a tile and a half tall, about 2.5 to 3 heads, by eye128 texels a tile; a standing Uma about 212 texels, 1.65 tiles, about four heads
LightReal light on the sprites, like a diorama (Octopath reworked its point lights to touch the pixel art)The scene's light and night grade on the quads; drawn light from the top front-left
DepthTilt-shift, parallax; depth of field as a PC settingTilt-shift and bloom (D18), off under the saver; the tilt-shift held at 0.8 so the rows units stand on stay sharp

Triangle Strategy's free camera was a deliberate cost: Square Enix says a tactics game must let the player see the field from every side, so every map had to look right from every angle. On a phone, a fixed camera keeps the board a one-hand read and spares building every map for every side, and the facing wedge and the forecast card (TC29) answer what rotation answers there, who's behind whom.

What follows for the sprites: four facings and no diagonals, each drawn once and seen only from its one angle. If 90° rotation ever arrives, the same four views still cover it: the game shows the view her facing makes against the camera, as Triangle Strategy must. uma-stage stretches each sprite quad by 1/cos 35° so the figure isn't squashed by the pitch and one texel lands on one device pixel at DPR 3; sprites are never scaled by a fraction, and never enlarged through a smoothing filter (Tactics Ogre Reborn's most criticised change).

Why four heads, and why redrawn. Triangle Strategy's director describes a search for "the right line" of deformation: the more heads tall a figure is, the smaller each pixel becomes and the more it reads as a shrunk illustration rather than a sprite, so they drew deformed figures first and added realistic light on top. Octopath II went the other way for a reason this game shares: its team made the characters taller so they sat right against sharper backgrounds and so each skill could have its own motion. This game sits between the two: its sprites are clean, antialiased anime at 256 texels (format.md §10), not hard pixel art, and its looks are adults in armor, so about four heads keeps the armor's plates and the long legs while the head (about 52 texels, 17 pt on the phone) still carries her face. The approved looks are about seven heads; shrinking one to 212 texels would lose the face, so each frame is redrawn at sprite size with the head enlarged, as Octopath's artists redrew from the illustrations.

LK7, facings. Four, each drawn, never mirrored: front (toward the camera, row-down), back (away, row-up), left and right (three-quarter, turned to image-left and image-right). They're v1's field facings; the board's N, S, E and W map to back, front, right and left (uma-stage's Facing). There's no separate battle facing on the board: the cut-in has its own keys (LK4). Drawing three views and mirroring the side would save a quarter of the frames, but anything on one side (a hair ornament, a braid, a crest, a forearm shield, the weapon hand) changes sides when flipped, and every approved look has such a detail.

LK8, readability at 70 pt. Each look's sprite keeps, at full size:

  • the head readable: hair shape, ears and accessory; the face a few clear marks (eyes, mouth);
  • her accent colour in two big areas at least (shoulders and boots on the armor; the jacket or hood on the street look);
  • the emblem as one clear mark of four to eight texels;
  • the template's silhouette: the armor's shoulder pads and boots must change her outline from the street look;
  • the tail always visible, in her colour;
  • the back view told apart at a glance: a hit from behind does +50% (TC23), so her back (hair, tail, the armor's back plate) must never be mistaken for her front; the wedge carries the rule, the sprite confirms it.

Two checks on every look's first frame: filled solid black at 70 pt, the silhouette still says which look it is (ears, shoulder pads and weapon at least two texels thick, no one-texel detail); and on the night board, it doesn't sink. Her palette passes through the scene's grade: a dark palette keeps a lit accent (crimson, gold) and a lighter rim on her lit side, top-left, so she doesn't sink into the terrain; a light one is fine as it is.

Per Uma, all five: about 220 frames, against v1's Traveler profile of 130 for one outfit. That's why the sets are tiered (LK12) and the combat set comes first.

Motion held back, on purpose. Octopath kept its frame counts low to keep the stepped feel of sprites, and the board's sprites do the same: a six-frame walk at about 100 ms a frame, a still stand with the game's breathing bob, and the big motion left to the cut-in.

8. Portraits and cut-in keys (LK10, LK11)

LK10, the approved looks are the portraits. Each 1024 × 1536 look shows full size where a scene or screen is about her (a visit, a bond scene, the deploy screen's selected Uma, the results), and a bust crop (head to chest, fixed per look) in the text box, the unit card and the timeline chip. In the context's look: her quarters bust in a cabin scene, her pilot bust at a post, her armor bust in battle. They're read from the Uma Platform app's universe looks (CA4) at run time, the same private way as master data's art, never in git; until a look exists, master data's bust or render stands in.

LK11, a few cut-in keys per Uma. Side view, combat armor, about 360 px tall in a 512 cell, light from the front: stance, melee, shot, launcher (the Back unit firing over her shoulder), hack, guard, hurt, kneel, victory, and strike with the helmet on (the visor down for the hit). Ten keys, drawn as stills; the cut-in moves them (slides, shakes, a flash), as uma-quest's fights do with their keys.

While a look isn't drawn, the game falls back: a missing board sprite uses her original-outfit chibi set from master data (D19), else her face card; a missing portrait uses master data's bust or render. Nothing waits on art.

LK13, who draws and how it flows.

  1. The looks (portraits): ChatGPT draws each Uma's five looks in the Character Art Studio, from her master data art and the five templates (the pilots' looks are the reference sheets), the owner approves, and pnpm art:push publishes them to Uma Character Art (CA4).
  2. Board sprites and cut-in keys: ChatGPT draws them from the approved looks, in uma-platform's sprite pipeline (pipeline.md), to this spec's profiles. The first frame of each look comes first: her front standing frame, checked on the board at 70 pt (LK8's two checks, the face readable) before the rest of the set is drawn; if the face doesn't read, the head grows toward three and a half heads for every look. The owner approves in the Studio; they publish in master data's private release, keyed by outfit and look.
  3. The game reads both at run time, picks the look by context (LK2) and falls back while one is missing.

The order: the pilots' combat set and cut-in keys first (the board), then their pilot-suit set (the ship's posts, CY16), quarters set (the cabins, CY14), street set (the world dioramas, Phase 5); then each lead's looks as the story names them.

10. What changes where (LK14)

LK14, the requests and the game's side.

  • To uma-platform (the request): a Tactics adult sprite profile (four heads, four facings, the actions of LK9), the helmet head patch (format.md §5's head box as a patch), sprite sets and keys keyed by outfit and look in master data's release, the Studio's Uma Cyberpunk categories for the five looks and the cut-in keys, and the universe looks readable by this game's API.
  • In this game, with the packets that first show each look: a look per screen and per scene (LK2), the portrait and bust from the universe looks with their fallbacks, the helmet head patch on the board, sealed maps (a map flag), and the cut-in playing the keys.
