Character looks and sprites: the authoring spec

Status: 2026-11-13 (D23, narrowed by D24). The owner asked for the character art in the Uma Platform app's Character Art (the Cyberpunk universe's approved looks of the first two pilot Umas) to inform the game's looks, and for a spec of how the character sprites are authored from that art and Triangle Strategy's camera. On reading it, the owner asked for a focused, image-only sprite request and questioned sprites for every outfit (D24): sprites are for combat only, as chibis, and every other look shows as its approved image. This page is the game's side, nameless: Umas are outfit ids, and the per-Uma notes are private (content/design/character-looks/). Its calls are LK1–LK14, each a recommendation, adopted until the owner changes one. The sprite format it builds on is uma-platform's (format.md, v1), unchanged. What ChatGPT draws is the combat sprites request. Visual page: previews/character-sprites.html.

The short answer

  • Five looks per Uma, each tied to where she is (LK1): Street when exploring worlds and cities, Quarters in her cabin and her evenings aboard, Pilot suit at her ship station, Combat armor on the battle board, and the helmet closed only when a map is sealed or for the cut-in's strike.
  • Sprites only for combat (LK1, LK4, D24). The battle board is the one place she's a small figure that walks and acts, so only the combat armor (helmet off, then on) gets sprites. Street, Quarters and Pilot suit show as the approved full-body images and their busts, which already exist.
  • The sprites are chibis (LK6): her approved chibi from the platform's sprite set, the same face, head and about three heads of height, re-dressed in her combat armor. The platform's format, cell and checks stay as they are, and a drawn pilot stands at the same scale as every other Uma still on her original chibi set.
  • One template per look, shared by every Uma (LK3): the same jacket cut, plugsuit, Modular Frame armor and Helmet, personalised by her palette, her emblem and her head (hair, ears, eyes, accessory). That's what the ten approved looks already do, and it's how a hundred Umas stay consistent and affordable.
  • The board's camera is ours, not Triangle Strategy's (LK5): Triangle Strategy's camera turns a full circle, which had its team build every map for every side. Ours is uma-stage's orthographic view, fixed at 35°, along the grid's axes, so a sprite needs four facings (front, back and true side profiles), never diagonals, and nothing rotates.
  • One combat set per Uma (LK9): 73 poses helmet off (the identity sheet, walking in four facings, reactions in four facings, a victory), and the same 73 with the helmet, made by editing only the head of the approved sheets.
  • ChatGPT generates images, nothing else (LK13): pose sheets, from the request and the private prompts. Cutting, alignment, the atlas, checks, publishing and the game's use come later (LK14).

1. What the approved looks establish

The Cyberpunk universe in Uma Character Art (published 2026-11-13, style "Hardline Anime", revision 3) holds ten approved full-body looks, 1024 × 1536: five looks each for the two pilot Umas (outfits 106801 and 112901).

Look (its id in the art)Its title thereThe template
streetStreetA cropped jacket or shell over a top with her emblem, cargo pants with knee pads, fingerless gloves, high-top sneakers
quartersQuarters: pajama shorts and crop topA crop top with her emblem, drawstring shorts, slippers
pilotShip operating: pilot suitA skintight plugsuit in her palette, line panels, clamps or knots at the hips, gloves, boots
armorCombat armor: Modular Frame 01Chest plate with her emblem, segmented abdomen, big shoulder pads, forearm gauntlets, thigh, knee and shin plates, armored boots; head bare

Across her five looks, these never change: her hair, ears, eyes, tail and signature head accessory (except under the helmet), her emblem (carried onto every outfit), and her palette. The tail is always out; the ears always show (the helmet houses them). Every look is asymmetric (a hair ornament on one side, a braid, a crest on one sleeve, a shield on one forearm), so sprites are never mirrored.

There's precedent in part: Octopath shows a character's second-job outfit only in battle and keeps her own clothes on the field, while Triangle Strategy keeps one sprite everywhere and changes it on a promotion. This game goes further, because its looks are the life aboard made visible: she dresses for where she is.

LK2, the context decides, a scene can override. The game picks by context: a world location is Street, her cabin and the ship's spots are Quarters, a post is Pilot suit, a mission is Combat. A scene or bond scene can name a look (look in the story format, live with the packet that first plays looks) when the story wants another, such as her pilot suit in a Bridge emergency at night.

The helmet rule (part of LK1): on the board the helmet stays off by default, because her face, hair and accessory are how she's told apart at 69 pt. It closes on sealed maps (a map flag, every Uma at once, so it's a visible "this place is hostile" signal), for the suit-up's last beat, and on the cut-in's strike. On a sealed map her identity rests on her helmet's palette, ear housings and emblem, her ring's colour, and her face on the timeline chip and unit card, which stay helmet-off. Her rig's Visor part (TC17) recolours the helmet's visor band, so the part reads on the helmet.

3. The template system (LK3)

LK3, five templates for every Uma, personalised three ways. The approved looks already work this way: both pilots wear the same jacket cut, plugsuit, Modular Frame and Helmet.

LayerShared by every UmaHers
TemplateThe look's cut and silhouette: the plates, the panels, the seams—
Palette—Three roles: base (the largest areas), accent (her identity colour, TC41's hue), trim (piping, buckles, glow); taken from her original outfit
EmblemWhere it sits on each template (chest, collar, forehead, shoulder)Its shape: one simple mark readable at a few texels
HeadEars in the helmet's housingsHer hair, ears, eyes, accessory and tail, unchanged across looks
MotifOne optional slot per template (a sleeve crest, a forearm shield, a belt bow)One detail from her original outfit, on one side

What it buys: a new Uma's five looks are one consistent family; a board of twelve armored Umas reads as a squad, each told apart by head and accent; and the art can be checked against a fixed list (does the emblem sit where the template puts it, is the accent hers).

4. Two kinds of character art (LK4)

LK4, sprites for the board, the approved images for everything else. Fire Emblem pairs its map sprites with separate battle animations and portraits, and Unicorn Overlord cuts from small map figures to side-on fighters. Triangle Strategy and Octopath draw their portraits apart from their sprites (a Triangle Strategy mod swaps one heroine's portrait on its own), and Octopath's sprites were converted by hand from the illustrations, finished to look best as sprites with stronger colours. This game does the same, with the cut-in's keys as the battle close-up.

5. The camera (LK5)

LK5, the board's camera is uma-stage's, fixed. Triangle Strategy's camera is a reference for the look, not the geometry:

Triangle StrategyThis game's board (uma-stage)
RotationA full 360° on the right stick, the big change from Octopath's fixed camera; its team built map parts for every direction and argued long over the map edgesNone (TC16, TC48: 90° steps only if a map ever needs them)
Tilt and zoomZoom on the shoulder buttons in battle and in town; the view tilts from near isometric to nearly top-downFixed pitch; a two-axis pan, no zoom in v1
ProjectionNot published; reads as a narrow-angle perspectiveOrthographic
PitchNot published; reads as about 30–40° at the default view (Final Fantasy Tactics' usual camera is about 26.5°)35°, fixed
Grid on screenAny angle, since the camera turnsAxis-aligned: rows run across the screen, the back row at the top
Facing as a ruleFour grid facings, chosen as a turn ends; a hit from behind is a criticalFour, the same (TC23: side +25%, back +50%), shown as a wedge on the base ring
Views drawnNot published; with a free camera, the view shown follows her facing against the camera's angleFront, back and true side profiles, each seen from one angle only
ScaleNot published; a unit about a tile and a half tall, about 2.5 to 3 heads, by eye128 texels a tile; a standing Uma about 208 texels, 1.6 tiles, about three heads
LightReal light on the sprites, like a diorama (Octopath reworked its point lights to touch the pixel art)The scene's light and night grade on the quads; drawn light from the top front-left
DepthTilt-shift, parallax; depth of field as a PC settingTilt-shift and bloom (D18), off under the saver; the tilt-shift held at 0.8 so the rows units stand on stay sharp

Triangle Strategy's free camera was a deliberate cost: Square Enix says a tactics game must let the player see the field from every side, so every map had to look right from every angle. On a phone, a fixed camera keeps the board a one-hand read and spares building every map for every side, and the facing wedge and the forecast card (TC29) answer what rotation answers there, who's behind whom.

What follows for the sprites: four facings and no diagonals, each drawn once and seen only from its one angle. If 90° rotation ever arrives, the same four views still cover it: the game shows the view her facing makes against the camera, as Triangle Strategy must. The sprites are drawn at eye level, like every platform chibi: uma-stage stands each one upright and stretches its quad by 1/cos 35°, so the figure isn't squashed by the pitch and one texel lands on one device pixel at DPR 3. Sprites are never scaled by a fraction, and never enlarged through a smoothing filter (Tactics Ogre Reborn's most criticised change).

Why chibi. Three reasons, each enough on its own:

  • The board is mixed for a long time. Every Uma whose combat set isn't drawn walks the board on her original chibi set (D19). A drawn pilot at the same three heads stands beside them at the same scale. A taller adult figure would jump out of the squad.
  • Triangle Strategy does it. Its units are deformed figures, about 2.5 to 3 heads by eye. Its director describes a search for "the right line" of deformation: the taller the figure, the smaller each pixel and the more it reads as a shrunk illustration, so they drew deformed figures and added believable light on top.
  • Her head carries her identity at 69 pt. A chibi's head is about a third of her height. It holds the hair, ears, accessory and face that tell twelve armored Umas apart.

The chibi also keeps the platform's format, its 14 checks and its prompt templates unchanged, and her approved chibi is the exact reference for the face. Octopath II made its figures taller to suit sharper backgrounds and give each skill its own motion. This board leaves the big motion to the cut-in, so it doesn't need that.

LK7, five views, each drawn, never mirrored. The key (three-quarter, turned to image-left: the identity reference) and the four the board plays:

  • front (toward the camera, row-down);
  • back (away, row-up);
  • left and right, which are true side profiles, as the owner asked for the platform's chibi sets (P105).

The board's N, S, E and W map to back, front, right and left (uma-stage's Facing). Drawing three views and mirroring the side would save a quarter of the frames, but anything on one side changes sides when flipped: a hair ornament, a braid, a crest, a forearm shield, the weapon hand. Every approved look has such a detail.

LK8, readability at 69 pt. Each sprite keeps, at full size:

  • the head readable: hair shape, ears and accessory; the face a few clear marks (eyes, mouth);
  • her accent colour in two big areas at least (the shoulders and shins or boots);
  • the emblem as one clear mark of four to eight texels;
  • the armor's silhouette: chunky shoulder pads and boots change her outline from her chibi's dress;
  • the tail always visible, in her colour;
  • the back view told apart at a glance: a hit from behind does +50% (TC23), so her back (hair, tail, the armor's back plate) must never be mistaken for her front; the wedge carries the rule, the sprite confirms it.

Two checks on every identity sheet:

  • The silhouette: filled solid black at 69 pt, it still reads as her in armor. Ears, shoulder pads and gauntlets are at least two texels thick, with no one-texel detail.
  • The night board: she doesn't sink into it. Her palette passes through the scene's grade. A dark palette keeps a lit accent (crimson, gold) and a lighter edge on her lit side, top-left. A light palette is fine as it is.

That's against v1's Traveler profile of 130 frames for one outfit. The board plays batch 1 today: standing stills and walking. Batch 2 waits for a board that shows reactions, and batch 3 for the first sealed map. Both are drawn now so the set is whole.

Motion held back, on purpose. Octopath kept its frame counts low to keep the stepped feel of sprites, and the board's sprites do the same: a six-frame walk at about 110 ms a frame (v1's timing), a still stand with the game's breathing bob, and the big motion left to the cut-in.

8. Portraits and cut-in keys (LK10, LK11)

LK10, the approved looks are the portraits. Each 1024 × 1536 look shows full size where a scene or screen is about her: a visit, a bond scene, her cabin (standing over its diorama), the deploy screen's selected Uma, the results. A bust crop (head to chest, fixed per look) shows in the text box, on her post's card, on the unit card and on the timeline chip. Each is in the context's look: her quarters bust in a cabin scene, her pilot bust at a post, her armor bust in battle. They're read from the Uma Platform app's universe looks (CA4) at run time, the same private way as master data's art, never in git. Until a look exists, master data's bust or render stands in.

LK11, a few cut-in keys per Uma, a separate request later. Side view, combat armor, about 360 px tall in a 512 cell, light from the front: stance, melee, shot, launcher (the Back unit firing over her shoulder), hack, guard, hurt, kneel, victory, and strike with the helmet on (the visor down for the hit). Ten keys, drawn as stills; the cut-in moves them (slides, shakes, a flash), as uma-quest's fights do with their keys. They aren't in the combat sprites request.

While a look isn't drawn, the game falls back:

  • a missing combat set: her original-outfit chibi set from master data (D19), else her face card;
  • a missing portrait: master data's bust or render.

Nothing waits on art.

LK13, ChatGPT generates images, nothing else.

  1. The looks (portraits): ChatGPT draws each Uma's five looks in the Character Art Studio, from her master data art and the five templates (the pilots' looks are the reference sheets). The owner approves them, and they're published to Uma Character Art (CA4). The pilots' are done.
  2. The combat sprites: ChatGPT generates the pose sheets of the request, using each Uma's private paste-ready prompts. Her reference board holds her approved chibi stills and her approved armor and helmet looks. The sheets are saved privately with their prompts.
  3. The owner's gates: the owner looks at the identity sheet (S1) before anything else is drawn, and at the walks (S2, S3) before the reactions. Any change is redrawn before the next step.
  4. Everything after the images is later (LK14): cutting the sheets into frames, alignment, the atlas, the checks, publishing, and the game's use.

10. What changes where (LK14)

LK14, one image request now; the integration later.

  • Now: the combat sprites request for ChatGPT, with the private prompts and reference boards in content/design/character-looks/. It asks for images only.
  • Later, once the sheets exist and pass the gates:
    • their export into the platform's format (format.md, unchanged);
    • a way to key a set by outfit and look in master data's release, beside today's chibi set;
    • the universe looks readable by this game's API.

    That's one uma-platform request under the change protocol, written then from what the sheets need. D23's draft of it is withdrawn (D24).

  • In this game, with the packets that first show each look:
    • a look per screen and per scene (LK2);
    • the portraits and busts from the universe looks, with their fallbacks;
    • the combat set on the board, the helmet sheets on sealed maps (a map flag);
    • the cut-in playing the keys.
